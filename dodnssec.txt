The DO's and DOn'ts of Post-Quantum DNSSEC

  There has been a lot of interest in recent months on the topic of
  post-Quantum cryptography (PQC) and DNSSEC. The development of quantum
  computers poses a threat to conventional cryptographic practices, where an
  adequately powerful quantum computer, a so-called "cryptographically
  relevant quantum computer" (CRQC), could be used derive the private key
  from exposed public information including the public key and encrypted
  text within a timeframe that could permit subsequent exploitation. In the
  context of DNSSEC, the risk lies in the potential for a CRQC to calculate
  this private key within the lifetime of the use of a DNSSEC key pair.

        In recent times there has been considerable interest in the
        development of so-called quantum computers. These systems exploit
        quantum mechanical phenomena where a unit of information is not a
        classical bit with a value of 1 or 0, but a qbit that is the
        superposition of its two basic states simultaneously. There are many
        sources of descriptions of quantum computers, so I'll not go into
        any further detail here, but suffice it to say that while there is
        much optimism that quantum computers will be refined in the coming
        years to the point where they are able to solve significant
        computational challenges, the current state of quantum computers is
        very early in its infancy! They represent the equivalent of a
        massively parallel processing, sometimes described as parallel
        processing at exponential scale.

        The engineering challenges with quantum computers are significant,
        and progress in constructing a quantum computer has so far been slow
        and extremely expensive. What's kept many projects going is the
        prospect that a significantly large quantum computer could solve a
        range of computational challenges that are simply beyond the
        practical reach of binary computers.

        Well in advance of the engineering challenge of constructing quantum
        computers, academic research into the properties of quantum
        computers highlighted the observation that quantum computing could
        be significantly faster in solving certain classes of problems than
        classical computers. In 1994 Peter Shor published an algorithm for
        finding the prime factors of an integer. This algorithm has
        compelling potential application in cryptography when the
        exponential speedup compared to best known classical (non-quantum)
        algorithms heralds a new era of cryptography. The implication is
        that cyphertext that is encrypted using RSA and ECC algorithms is
        susceptible to being broken once quantum computers achieve the
        necessary scale and reliability goals. This computer of necessary
        scale and reliability is termed a Cryptographically Relevant Quantum
        Computer (CRQC). When that may happen is literally anyone's guess,
        but the more money that gets pumped into finding solutions to the
        engineering issues of quantum computers, the earlier that date will
        be. The year 2030 has been talked about, and it is not considered to
        be completely crazy date, even though it's well on the optimistic
        side. This date is well within a two-decade horizon of keeping a
        secret, so it you want to ensure that what you are encrypting today
        remains a secret for the next twenty years, then it is prudent to
        assume that quantum computers will be used to try and break this
        secret sometime within that twenty-year period. So, even though
        capable quantum computers are yet to be built, we need consider the
        use of quantum-resistant cryptographic algorithms today in certain
        areas where long-held integrity of the encryption process is
        important.

        The present risk for encryption lies in an attacker performing data
        capture now, in anticipation of being able to post-process it later
        with a CRQC. There is even an acronym for this, Harvest Now, Decrypt
        Later (HNDL).

        Cryptography is also used for digital signatures, where the
        signature is used to establish the authenticity of signed message
        and the veracity of its author. Compromise of the key pair would
        allow an attacker to impersonate the author. Here the period of
        vulnerability is related to the period of use of the key pair used
        to generate digital signatures. The longer the period of use the
        longer the time an attacker has to calculate the private key.

  DNSSEC key lifetimes for DNS top-level domains (tlds) were studied in an
  ICANN publication in 2022 (Observing DNSSEC Key Lifecycles", Ed Lewis,
  OCTO-035 <https://www.icann.org/en/system/files/files/octo-035-18jul22-en.pdf>).
  Key management practices appeared to vary with respect to the lifetime of
  use, but a first level generalisation this study reported a zone's
  Key-Signing Key (KSK) being used for a period of one or more years, and
  the Zone Signing Key (ZSK) being used for a period of one or more months.

  A first line response to the CRQC risk is to use an operational practice
  of regular key rotation, introducing a new ZSK every month, and a new KSK
  every year, or even every six months. These numbers are perhaps slightly
  more frequent in terms of key rotation for KSKs as observed in the ICANN
  study, but they do not represent a major change in the anticipated
  longevity of existing keys in the face of the current quantum risk.
  However, the situation is not static, and if the development of quantum
  computers is rapid, then this practice of using short key lifetimes will
  not produce a large amount of leeway in terms of the need to switch to a
  PQC in your DNSSEC configuration.

  That means we should now look at the potential impacts of quantum
  cryptography on DNSSEC.

  One common characteristic of many post-quantum cryptography algorithms is
  that they require significantly larger key sizes than commonly used
  "pre-quantum" public key algorithms. For example, comparing the key and
  signature sizes of DNSSEC algorithm 13 (ECDSA P-256 with SHA 256) with
  DNSSEC algorithm 18 (ML-DSA-44) we have the following comparison:
  
                 Public Key    Private Key      Signature
    ECDSA P-256     32 B           32 B             65 B
    ML-DSA-44    1,312 B        2,560 B          2,420 B

  The implication is that when you attach a DNSSEC signature generated by a
  post-quantum crypto algorithm to a DNS response then the size of the
  response gets a whole lot larger.

  For example, when a DNS stub resolver queries a recursive resolver for a
  name signed with ECDSA P-256 and requests the signature record by setting
  the DNSSEC OK bit in the query, then the response includes this 65-byte
  signature. At 191 bytes, this response fits easily into an unfragmented
  UCP packet. When we repeat this query for a name signed with the
  post-quantum ML-DSA-44 algorithm, the response is 2,551 bytes.

  This latter query elicited a truncated response from the recursive
  resolver over UDP, and the stub resolver had to open up a TCP session to
  repeat the query over TCP. Instead of a 2-packet query/response exchange
  over UDP, the entire DNS transaction took 10 packets, including the
  truncation signal from the initial UDP exchange, the TCP opening handshake
  and the TCP session completion packet exchange added to the query and
  response packets.

Transport for Large DNS Payloads

  Why not just use conventional IP packet fragmentation and stick with using
  UDP? The maximal size of an IP packet is 65,536 bytes, and we would
  certainly be able to use ML-DSA 44 and presumably all other candidate
  post-quantum crypto signature algorithms in DNS over UDP by allowing the
  network layer to fragment such large packets (or fragmentation at source
  in the case of IPv6) and reassemble the fragments at the destination.

  The problem is that IP packet fragmentation is unreliable and risky (see
  the article "DNS XL" for details and measurement data), and the general
  consensus in the DNS (and in other parts of the IP architecture) is that
  the downsides of IP packet fragmentation unreliability in both IPv4 and
  IPv6 outweigh any residual benefits of having the IP network layer manage
  large packet payloads through fragmentation.

  The original DNS specification effectively ignored this issue by simply
  limiting DNS payloads when using UDP to 512 octets or less. This was
  modified with the introduction of Extension Mechanisms for DNS (EDNS, RFC
  6891), and this extension mechanism includes a requestor's UDP payload
  size, defined as the largest UDP payload that can be reassembled and
  delivered in the requestor's network stack. Up to around 2019, the general
  guidance for DNS resolvers and servers was that: "A good compromise may be
  the use of an EDNS maximum payload size of 4096 octets as a starting
  point." (RFC 6891, Section 6.2.5).

  However, extensive measurements of IP fragmentation performance,
  particularly when using UDP over IPv6 raised serious doubts as to the
  robustness of IP packet fragmentation. To quote from the DNS Flag Day 2020
  web site (<https://www.dnsflagday.net/2020/>): "IP fragmentation is
  unreliable on the Internet today, and can cause transmission failures when
  large DNS messages are sent via UDP. Even when fragmentation does work, it
  may not be secure; it is theoretically possible to spoof parts of a
  fragmented DNS message, without easy detection at the receiving end." The
  recommendation from this initiative is to use an EDNS UDP buffer size of
  1,232 bytes, which was intended to avoid IP fragmentation on nearly all
  current IP networks.

  How well do recursive resolvers follow this recommendation to use a EDNS
  UDP buffer size of 1,232 in their queries? A distribution of these buffer
  sizes in DNS in today's Internet is shown in Figure 1. This Is a data set
  gathered by the APNIC Measurement platform over a 7-day period ending on 3
  November 2026, collecting a total of some 36 billion queries gathered from
  some 245M end users who were enrolled in this measurement.
  
    <https://ispcol.potaroo.net/2026-11/dodnssec-f1.png>
    Figure 1 - Distribution of EDNS UDP Buffer Sizes in DNS Queries

  Clearly, most of the queries in this data set use a UDP buffer size of
  1,410 bytes, which appears to allow a DNS payload, a UDP transport header
  and an IPv4 or IPv6 IP header to fit within a 1,500-byte IP packet. (In a
  similar measurement exercise conducted in September 2020, 83% of some 875M
  queries used a UDP Buffer Size value of 4,096 bytes
  (<https://ispcol.potaroo.net/2020-11/xldns.html>), so there has been a
  considerable change in this behavioural profile in the ensuing 6 years.

  If we take a DNS payload size of 1,472 bytes in IPv4 and 1,452 in IPv6 as
  being the equivalent of a 1,500-byte IP packet, then 75.2% of queries are
  using a buffer size value that it likely to avoid IP fragmentation in DNS
  over UDP responses.

  Of course, that figure implies that 24.8% of queries indicate that these
  recursive resolvers can accept an IP fragmented DNS response whose total
  DNS payload size is larger than 1,500 bytes. It is possible that this is
  indeed the case, but a more likely explanation is that the recursive
  resolver is simply using a buffer size setting derived from a simple cut
  and paste from RFC 6891.

  However, it's not a bad as this may sound, as it's highly improbable that
  this distribution of UDP buffer sizes in queries would result in around
  one quarter of all responses being passed back with IP-fragmented UDP when
  an authoritative server is attempting to respond with a large DNS
  response. The server has its own maximum DNS UDP payload size as well, and
  the minimum of the query's UDP buffer size and the server's UDP buffer
  size is used to determine if the server will generate a UDP response or
  respond with a truncated response (thereby signalling that the querier
  should re-try the query over TCP). As long as authoritative servers are
  behaving conservatively and applying a conservative UDP buffer size limit
  to its responses then IP fragmentation unreliability can be avoided.

  The implication here is that it's highly likely that mostly all large DNS
  responses in the public DNS would cause a server to generate a DNS
  response over UDP with the truncation flag set, which would then trigger a
  re-query over TCP.
  
  In this case, the large response will be generated when replying to a
  query from a recursive resolver where the query name has been signed with
  a post-quantum cryptographic algorithm, and the query has the DNSSEC OK
  (DO) bit set in the query. A similar behaviour exists for a query to a
  non-existent name in a DNSSEC signed zone, where the negative response
  will contain two signature records, one for the zone's start of authority
  record, and the other for the spanning NSEC record demonstrating
  non-existence of the queried name.

Setting the DNSSEC OK bit

  A DNS recursive resolver signals to a server, or a stub resolver signals
  to a recursive resolver, that it wants the DNSSEC signature (that has been
  bound to the queried name) attached to the response by setting the DNSSEC
  OK bit in the query. The response will then have the RRSIG resource record
  set (RRset) associated with the queried name, if the zone is a
  DNSSEC-signed zone.

  This means that if a stub resolver is not going to perform DNSSEC
  validation, then the DO bit is clear in the query to the recursive
  resolver. If the stub resolver is not interested in having the recursive
  resolver perform validation, then this bit should also be clear in the
  queries sent by the recursive resolver to authoritative servers when
  resolving this name. But that's not the case. The relevant specification
  text here is in RFC 4035, which states: "A security-aware resolver MUST
  include an EDNS (RFC 2671) OPT pseudo-RR with the DO (RFC 3225) bit set
  when sending queries." If the initiating query to this resolver does not
  have the DO bit set, this recursive DNSSEC-aware resolver will still issue
  the query to the authoritative server with the DO bit set, but remove
  DNSSEC RRsets before returning the data to the original client.

  It appears that the reason for this resolver behaviour is to avoid
  inadvertent DNSSEC stripping. If a security-aware resolver always asks for
  the DNSSEC RRset through the DO flag, then it can always use the cached
  entry for subsequent queries, stripping these RRsets from the response if
  the query does not have the DO bit set. The alternative approach is to
  cache the query's DO bit setting with the response, and if the query has
  the DO bit set and the cached entry does not, then the resolver would need
  to re-query with the DO bit set and cache this new result.

  In today's network, how often is the DO bit set in queries?

      QType='A'              18,129,250,428    10,909,410,674    60.2%
      Non-Duplicate Queries   2,371,625,460     1,443,544,917    60.9%
      
  These numbers are from a 1-week measurement for the period ending 3
  November using the APNIC measurement platform which undertook measurements
  from a broad sampling of end users as performed by Google's Ad delivery
  system. The first row reflects all queries received for unique domain
  names. The second row reflect all queries using the 'A' query type, and
  the third row removes all duplicate queries, which is the equivalent of
  one query per end user. The relative use of the DO bit is constant across
  these classifications.

  We can also look at the ratio of users that are performing DNSSEC
  validation. The APNIC Labs report on DNSSEC validation</a> rates for this
  same period (<https://stats.labs.apnic.net/dnssec/XA>) is 40% of users.
  These 40% of users are located behind recursive resolvers that perform
  DNSSEC validation and will not resolve a name which cannot be validated. A
  further 8.5% of users are located behind DNS resolution environments that
  also attempt DNSSEC validation, but when they receive a SERVFAIL response
  from a DNSSEC-validating resolver, they appear to re-query using a
  resolver that does not perform DNSSEC validation. This would suggest that
  some 48% of users are sending queries to a recursive resolver that is
  security-aware and would be expected to set the DO bit in their queries to
  authoritative servers. Our 60% result of users sending DO bit queries
  indicates that a further 12% of users do not appear to be performing
  DNSSEC validation (there are no queries for DNSKEY records), yet the
  resolvers these users are using are setting the DO bit in their queries.

Is this a problem?

  Does this figure of 12% users whose DNS resolvers appear to be making
  gratuitous DO queries to authoritative servers present a problem for the
  DNS infrastructure?

  There are three major factors to consider here.

  The first is the impact of caching. We've already seen that some 48% of
  users generate queries that appear to trigger the fetching of DNSSEC
  signatures, but there is a critical difference between the APNIC
  measurement and the conventional operation of the DNS. DNS recursive
  resolvers operate local caches, and query traffic appears to have a high
  level of self-similarity. The APNIC measurement uses unique labels to
  specifically prevent local caches from absorbing such queries before they
  get to the authoritative servers. That implies that while it may entail
  some additional packets and delay to retrieve the DNSSEC signatures, the
  responses are held in the local cache.

  Secondly, at least for the present, the overall majority of stub resolvers
  do not directly validate DNS responses. They leave that task to the
  recursive resolver and take their hint from the AD bit set in the response
  as an indication that the resolver has successfully validated the
  response.

  Thirdly, if you look at the profile of queried names on the Internet, few
  of these queried names are from DNSSEC-signed zones. The <a
  href="https://stats.labs.apnic.net/cfdnssecdata/?w=1&a=0">data</a> from
  the Cloudflare 1.1.1.1 recursive resolver indicates that only some 2% of
  queried names are DNSSEC-signed in the first place.

DNSSEC Signals

  Let's quickly review the signalling mechanisms used in the DNS to control
  DNSSEC functions.

    1. The DO Bit (DNSSEC OK)
    
    This bit is defined in EDNS0 extension OPT pseudo-resource record,
    rather than the main 12-byte DNS header. A DNS resolver or client may
    set this bit in a query, and it informs the upstream name server or
    recursive resolver: "I am DNSSEC-aware. You are allowed to include
    DNSSEC resource records (like RRSIG, DNSKEY, and DS) in your response."
    If the DO bit is 0 (or EDNS0 is omitted from the query), a
    security-aware server will usually withhold DNSSEC signature records to
    keep the response small (under 512 bytes). Specifically, the DO bit does
    not say "Please perform DNSSEC validation on any response you may
    generate." That signal, in its negative form, is left to the CD
    bit.

    2. CD Bit (Checking Disabled)

    This is bit 11 of the standard DNS message header (repurposed from an
    originally reserved Z bit). A stub resolver may set this bit in a query
    to a recursive resolver. It tells the recursive name server: "Do not
    block or strip out data if DNSSEC validation fails. Send me the records
    anyway."

    A validating stub resolver (running locally on an end-host) might set CD
    to 1 so it can fetch the cryptographic signature data (normally through
    using the DO bit) and perform the validation check itself, rather than
    trusting an upstream resolver.

    If the CD bit is clear (0) a DNSSEC-validating recursive resolver will
    return the SERVFAIL response code if the recursive resolver's validation
    fails.<br> <br>

    3. AD Bit (Authentic Data)

    This is bit 10 of the standard DNS message header (repurposed from an
    originally reserved Z bit). A recursive resolver will set this bit in a
    response as a signal that every resource record in the answer section
    has been cryptographically verified via DNSSEC and is authentic.

    Stub resolvers should only trust the AD=1 response if they have a
    secure, trusted and authenticated channel to that recursive resolver.


  Summary

    Bit       Name     Role    Purpose
    DO     DNSSEC OK  Query    Requests DNSSEC records (RRSIG, NSEC)
                               attached to a response
    CD     Checking   Query    Requests the resolver to bypass validation
           Disabled            and return data anyway
    AD     Authentic  Response Records that the returned data passed the 
           Data                recursive resolver's DNSSEC validation


  There are a number of consequences of this definition of these DNSSEC signals:
  
    - A stub resolver cannot directly query a recursive resolver's
      capabilities to detect if it performs DNSSEC validation of DNSSEC
      signed response.

    - A stub resolver may set the CD and DO bits in its query to indicate to
      a recursive resolver that it intends to perform DNSSEC validation
      itself and the recursive resolver should return a response
      irrespective of whether it would pass a local validation test or not.

    - The stub resolver may receive a response that has the AD bit set even
      when the CD bit is set. This seeming contradiction is clarified in RFC
      3665. The AD bit is set in a response when a resolver has verified the
      DNSSEC signatures accompanying the records in the Answer and Authority
      section. If the locally cached data has been verified already, then it
      will set the AD bit to indicate this, irrespective of the CD bit
      setting.

DNS over TCP

  It's not just the volume of these apparently gratuitous DO enabled
  queries. Does the introduction of post-quantum crypto signatures in DNSSEC
  pose a significant additional burden for DNS infrastructure, as these DO
  queries will entail a TCP transaction for such signatures? Presumably when
  the case for using post-Quantum algorithms is abundantly clear due to the
  availability of sufficiently powerful quantum computers then we might
  anticipate the entire DNSSEC space turning to use such algorithms. The
  inference is that a large proportion of DNS responses (60% on current
  numbers) would need to be carried by a failover to TCP.

  In such a scenario, if such a high proportion of DNS queries are being
  made over UDP only to be redirected to use TCP, then why start with UDP at
  all? Why wouldn't we just go straight to TCP when making a query with the
  DO but set? That scenario is hypothetical today, but there is a body of
  opinion that the development of quantum computers is quick enough for this
  to become a reality in just a few more years.

Should we change the DO bit behaviour?

  Where is all this leading?

  I'm a co-author of an internet draft (titled "No DO for you"
  (<https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-wagope-dnsop-do-bit/>) that
  proposes that "non-DNSEC validating resolvers clear the DO bit in their
  queries as they do not (necessarily) need the DNSSEC records. This
  approach may help mitigate the number of TCP connections that
  authoritative servers need to handle, but at the cost of additional
  complexity and number of queries." This behaviour would entail the
  resolver caching both the response and the DO bit setting used in the
  query that generated the response. If the cached response was obtained
  with the DO bit cleared and the resolver subsequently received a query
  with the DO bit set, it should perform a re-query with the DO bit set and
  load the response into its cache.

  This is a small tweak to DNS resolver behaviour that has the potential to
  remove some of the load of fetching DNSSEC signatures when they will not
  be used, and in the case of post-Quantum signatures avoiding the overhead
  of performing a TCP query in such gratuitous circumstances.

  On the other hand, there a lingering concern that the DNS is already
  overloaded with tweaks both small and large already
  (<https://www.ietf.org/blog/herding-dns-camel/>) and adding more such
  tweaks doesn't necessarily improve the robustness of the DNS. The
  continuous addition of such tweaks tends to make the DNS more fragile and
  less robust. TCP is an integral part of the DNS specification and taking
  such measures to avoid using TCP doesn't appear to make much sense.

  I believe I share my co-authors uncertainly here about the appropriate way
  to proceed, and the draft proposing to clear the DO bit in queries
  contains the following introduction:
  
    Editor note: THIS IS A WORKING IDEA FOR DISCUSSION.

    It might be the best idea ever... or the worst idea ever. The authors
    really cannot decide (or even agree among themselves), but we have
    written this up as an ID to share it with the WG. Please understand that
    we are simply floating the idea; if it turns out that it is the worse
    idea ever, we'll happily drop it and save ourselves a bunch of work! :-)
